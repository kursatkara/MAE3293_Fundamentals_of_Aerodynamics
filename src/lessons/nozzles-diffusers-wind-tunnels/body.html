Section 10.2

Governing equations for quasi-one-dimensional flow

In truly one-dimensional flow the area is constant and p, ρ, T, u vary only with x. In quasi-one-dimensional flow the area also varies, A = A(x), but the flow properties are still assumed uniform over each cross section: p = p(x), ρ = ρ(x), T = T(x), u = u(x). This is an approximation; the real flow in a variable-area duct is three-dimensional. For slowly varying ducts it predicts nozzle performance remarkably well.

Apply the integral conservation laws to a control volume bounded by two cross sections, A1 and A2, and the duct wall. The wall is a streamline surface, so \(\vec V\cdot d\vec S=0\) there. For steady, inviscid, adiabatic flow:

\[ \rho_1u_1A_1=\rho_2u_2A_2 \tag{10.1} \]
\[ p_1A_1+\rho_1u_1^2A_1+\int_{A_1}^{A_2}p\,dA=p_2A_2+\rho_2u_2^2A_2 \tag{10.5} \]
\[ h_1+\frac{u_1^2}{2}=h_2+\frac{u_2^2}{2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad h_0=\text{const} \tag{10.9} \]

The \(\int p\,dA\) term is the pressure force of the duct wall on the gas. It is the only new term compared with the normal-shock equations, and it requires A(x).

Together with p = ρRT and h = cpT, these five equations determine the five unknowns ρ2, p2, u2, T2, h2. A direct solution takes substantial algebra. The differential forms below lead to the physical insight much faster.

Derivation of the integral forms (notes pp. 1–3)
1

Continuity, \(\oint_S\rho\vec V\cdot d\vec S=0\) for steady flow. On face a, \(\vec V\cdot d\vec S=-u_1\,dS\); on face c, \(+u_2\,dS\); on face b (the wall) it is zero. With uniform properties over each face:

\[ -\rho_1u_1A_1+\rho_2u_2A_2=0 \]
2

The x-component of momentum, \(\oint_S(\rho\vec V\cdot d\vec S)u=-\oint_S(p\,d\vec S)_x\). On the wall, \((d\vec S)_x=-dA\), so the wall contributes \(-\int_{A_1}^{A_2}p(-dA)\):

\[ -\rho_1u_1^2A_1+\rho_2u_2^2A_2=-\left[-p_1A_1-\int_{A_1}^{A_2}p\,dA+p_2A_2\right] \]
3

Energy, \(\oint_S\rho\left(e+\tfrac{V^2}{2}\right)\vec V\cdot d\vec S=-\oint_S p\vec V\cdot d\vec S\). Divide by \(\rho_1u_1A_1=\rho_2u_2A_2\) and use h = e + p/ρ:

\[ \frac{p_1}{\rho_1}+e_1+\frac{u_1^2}{2}=\frac{p_2}{\rho_2}+e_2+\frac{u_2^2}{2} \]

Applied to an infinitesimal control volume of length dx, the same three laws give the differential forms:

\[ d(\rho uA)=0 \tag{10.14} \]
\[ dp=-\rho u\,du \tag{10.18} \]
\[ dh+u\,du=0 \tag{10.19} \]

Equation (10.18) is the Euler equation: the momentum equation for steady, inviscid flow. It holds for quasi-one-dimensional flow even though A varies, because the area terms cancel against continuity.

Derivation of the Euler equation (notes p. 4)
1

Momentum on the slab between x and x + dx, with \(\int p\,dA\approx p\,dA\):

\[ pA+\rho u^2A+p\,dA=(p+dp)(A+dA)+(\rho+d\rho)(u+du)^2(A+dA) \]

Drop products of differentials:

\[ A\,dp+Au^2d\rho+\rho u^2dA+2\rho uA\,du=0 \tag{10.16} \]
2

Expand continuity and multiply by u:

\[ u^2A\,d\rho+\rho uA\,du+\rho u^2dA=0 \tag{10.17} \]
3

Subtract (10.17) from (10.16): \(A\,dp+\rho uA\,du=0\), which is (10.18).

Section 10.2 · Physical characteristics

The area–velocity relation

Divide continuity by ρuA: \(\frac{d\rho}{\rho}+\frac{du}{u}+\frac{dA}{A}=0\). For flow without shocks (isentropic flow), \(dp/d\rho=a^2\), so the Euler equation becomes \(\frac{d\rho}{\rho}=-M^2\frac{du}{u}\). Eliminating dρ/ρ:

\[ \frac{dA}{A}=\left(M^2-1\right)\frac{du}{u} \tag{10.25} \]
The same area change does opposite things in subsonic and supersonic flow. Subsonic: a converging duct speeds the flow up and a diverging duct slows it down. Supersonic: a diverging duct speeds the flow up and a converging duct slows it down. Sonic: dA = 0, so M = 1 can occur only at a minimum area, the throat. As M → 0, Au = const, the incompressible continuity equation.

Section 10.3 · Nozzle flows

The area–Mach number relation

Apply continuity between the sonic section A* (where M = 1 and u* = a*) and any other section A: \(\rho^*u^*A^*=\rho uA\). Using \(\rho_0/\rho=\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}\), \(\rho^*/\rho_0=\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}\) and \(M^{*2}=(u/a^*)^2=\frac{(\gamma+1)M^2}{2+(\gamma-1)M^2}\):

\[ \left(\frac{A}{A^*}\right)^2=\frac{1}{M^2}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}} \tag{10.32} \]

The Mach number at any location in the duct depends only on the ratio of the local area to the sonic area. Every A/A* > 1 has two solutions, one subsonic and one supersonic. Which one the flow takes depends on the pressures at the ends of the duct.

Section 10.3 · Figures 10.12–10.16

A converging-diverging nozzle and its back pressure

A reservoir at p0, T0 feeds a nozzle with throat area At and exit area Ae; the nozzle exhausts into a back pressure pB. With p0 fixed, lowering pB moves the flow through a sequence of regimes. Three back pressures divide them:

  • pe1: the throat just reaches M = 1 and the flow returns to subsonic speed in the diverging section, from the subsonic root of (10.32) at Ae/At.
  • pe5: the supersonic flow ends in a normal shock standing exactly at the exit plane.
  • pe6: the design pressure, from the supersonic root of (10.32). The flow is isentropic and supersonic all the way to the exit.

Once the throat is sonic, nothing downstream can change the flow upstream of it: the mass flow is choked at \(\dot m=\rho^*u^*A_t\). Lowering pB further changes only the flow downstream of the throat.

The choked mass flow in terms of reservoir conditions
1

At the sonic throat, \(\dot m=\rho^*u^*A_t=\rho^*a^*A_t\), with \(\rho^*=\rho_0\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}\), \(a^*=a_0\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac12}\), \(\rho_0=\frac{p_0}{RT_0}\) and \(a_0=\sqrt{\gamma RT_0}\).

2

Combine:

\[ \dot m=\frac{p_0A_t}{\sqrt{T_0}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}} \]

The mass flow of a choked nozzle is proportional to p0At and inversely proportional to \(\sqrt{T_0}\). It does not depend on the back pressure or on the diverging section.

3

Across a normal shock T0 and \(\dot m\) are unchanged, so the same expression written at a sonic reference area on each side gives \(p_{0,1}A_1^*=p_{0,2}A_2^*\). The total-pressure loss enlarges the sonic area downstream of the shock.

Reading the pressure plot

  • Pressure falls wherever the flow accelerates, subsonic or supersonic: in the converging section, and in the diverging section once the flow is supersonic.
  • Pressure jumps across a normal shock, then rises further as the subsonic flow behind it decelerates in the diverging section.
  • A subsonic jet leaves at the back pressure, pe = pB. A supersonic jet can leave above or below pB and adjusts outside the nozzle through oblique shocks (p rises) or expansion fans (p falls).
  • Once the nozzle is choked, the pressure in the converging section, and the mass flow, no longer change with back pressure.
  • The Mach number, pressure and temperature at each section depend only on A/A* and on whether the flow there is subsonic or supersonic. The shape of the nozzle only decides where along x a given area, and so a given shock strength, occurs. Switch between the contoured and conical diverging sections to see this.

A convergent-divergent nozzle with Ae/At = is fed from a reservoir at p0 = atm and exhausts at pe = atm. A normal shock stands in the diverging section. Find its location (As/At), the Mach number ahead of it, and the exit Mach number.

The trick. The shock location is unknown, so neither p0,2 nor A2* is known. Their product is: the mass flow is the same on both sides, so p0,1A1* = p0,2A2*, with A1* = At. Multiply the exit pressure by the exit area:

\[ \frac{p_eA_e}{p_{0,1}A_t}=\frac{p_e}{p_{0,2}}\,\frac{A_e}{A_2^*} \]

The right side depends only on Me, so it can be inverted in closed form:

\[ M_e^2=-\frac{1}{\gamma-1}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{(\gamma-1)^2}+\frac{2}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}\left(\frac{p_{0,1}A_t}{p_eA_e}\right)^2} \]

Then p0,2/p0,1 follows from pe/p0,2, the normal-shock total-pressure ratio gives the Mach number ahead of the shock, and the area–Mach relation gives where that Mach number occurs.

Diffusers and supersonic wind tunnels

A diffuser slows a flow to low speed with as large a static-pressure rise, and as small a total-pressure loss, as possible. The ideal supersonic diffuser is a converging-diverging duct run in reverse: supersonic flow decelerates isentropically to M = 1 at the throat and then subsonically, with p0,2 = p0,1. In practice the oblique waves and boundary layer make this impossible. A real diffuser decelerates the flow through a pattern of reflected oblique shocks and a weak normal shock near the throat, so p0,2 < p0,1 and s2 > s1.

A supersonic wind tunnel must supply a reservoir pressure p0 high enough to drive the test-section Mach number Me against the back pressure pB. The diffuser lowers that requirement. Compare three layouts:

  • Nozzle exhausting directly to the atmosphere (Figure 10.18): the exit must be at design, pe = pB, so p0 = pB(p0/pe).
  • Constant-area duct with a normal shock at its exit (Figure 10.19): the shock raises the pressure by p2/p1, so p0 is lower by that factor.
  • Diffuser with a second throat (Figure 10.20): during start-up a normal shock passes through the test section, so the second throat must swallow the reduced total pressure behind it: \(A_{t,2}/A_{t,1}=p_{0,1}/p_{0,2}\). Once started, the shock sits in the diffuser at a lower Mach number, and its loss is smaller.

Section 10.5 · Supersonic wind tunnels

Starting a wind tunnel: swallowing the shock

When the tunnel is switched on, p0/pB rises from 1. First the whole tunnel is subsonic. Once the nozzle throat chokes, a normal shock forms in the nozzle and moves downstream as p0/pB rises. The test section reaches its design Mach number only after this shock has passed through the test section and through the second throat into the diverging part of the diffuser. The shock has then been swallowed and the tunnel is started.

The strongest shock, and the largest total-pressure loss, occurs while the shock stands in the test section at Me. The second throat must pass the full mass flow at that reduced total pressure, so it must be at least

\[ \frac{A_{t,2}}{A_{t,1}}\ge\frac{p_{0,1}}{p_{0,2}}\bigg|_{\text{normal shock at }M_e} \]

If the second throat is smaller, it chokes first. The shock then stays in the nozzle, at the Mach number whose total-pressure ratio equals At,1/At,2, and raising p0 cannot move it: the tunnel is unstarted. Once started, the shock sits in the diffuser at a lower Mach number, so the tunnel can run at a lower p0/pB than it needed to start. Lowering p0/pB below the running minimum makes the shock jump back upstream.

Section 10.6

Viscous flow: shock–boundary-layer interaction inside nozzles

The quasi-one-dimensional model puts a plane normal shock across the whole duct. In a real nozzle a boundary layer grows along the walls, and its inner part is subsonic. The pressure rise of the shock travels upstream through that subsonic layer, so the boundary layer thickens ahead of the shock and, for strong enough shocks, separates.

The thickened or separated layer turns the outer supersonic flow into itself, so an oblique shock, the front leg, forms ahead of the main shock. The front leg and a rear leg meet the nearly normal central shock at a triple point, and the pattern takes the shape of the Greek letter λ: a lambda shock. Behind it the flow near the wall is separated, the effective flow area shrinks, and the pressure recovers more gradually than the one-dimensional jump.

Consequences for practice:

  • The shock position, and the wall pressure downstream of it, differ from the one-dimensional prediction. The interactives on this page are the inviscid limit.
  • In strongly overexpanded rocket nozzles, for example a large-expansion engine at sea level, the flow separates from the wall inside the nozzle. Unsteady separation can produce large side loads.
  • In wind-tunnel diffusers the same interaction replaces the single weak normal shock with a train of oblique and λ shocks, which is why a real diffuser recovers less total pressure than the ideal one.
