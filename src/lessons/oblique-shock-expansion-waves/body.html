A supersonic flow that is turned into itself forms an oblique shock; a flow turned away from itself forms an expansion fan. This lesson builds both from the conservation laws, then combines them into shock-expansion theory to find the lift and drag of the flat plate, the wedge, and the diamond-wedge airfoil. Section 9.1 · Introduction

Supersonic flow over a corner

A normal shock makes a 90° angle with the upstream flow. In general a shock makes an oblique angle with the flow; these are oblique shock waves, and the normal shock is the special case where the wave angle is 90°. Supersonic flows also contain expansion waves, across which the pressure decreases continuously.

The flow at a wall must stay tangent to the wall. At a concave corner the wall turns up through the deflection angle θ, so every streamline turns up, into the main bulk of the flow. At a convex corner the wall turns down, and the streamlines turn away from the flow.

Whenever a supersonic flow is turned into itself, an oblique shock wave forms: M drops discontinuously and p, ρ, T jump up. Whenever it is turned away from itself, a centered expansion fan forms at the corner: M rises and p, ρ, T fall continuously. The expansion wave is the direct antithesis of a shock.

Section 9.1 · Source of the waves

Mach waves: why the waves are oblique

Consider a small source of disturbance, a “beeper,” moving through a stagnant gas and emitting sound pulses. Each pulse spreads as a circle at the speed of sound a. When the beeper moves subsonically (V < a), it always stays inside its family of circles and the waves run ahead of it. When it moves supersonically (V > a), it outruns its own waves. The circles then pile up along a straight envelope BC, the Mach wave, tangent to every circle.

In time t the beeper travels Vt from A to B while the pulse emitted at A spreads to radius at. The right triangle ABC gives the Mach angle μ:

\[ \sin\mu = \frac{a\,t}{V\,t} = \frac{a}{V} = \frac{1}{M}, \qquad \mu = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{M}\right) \tag{9.1} \]

If the disturbances are stronger than sound waves, the envelope becomes an oblique shock at angle β > μ. The physical mechanism is the same. A Mach wave is the limiting case of an oblique shock: an infinitely weak one.

Section 9.2

Oblique shock relations

Split the upstream velocity V1 into a component u1 normal to the shock and a component w1 tangential to it; do the same downstream. β is the shock (wave) angle and θ the deflection angle, both measured from the upstream flow direction. Apply the integral conservation laws to a control volume around the shock for steady, inviscid, adiabatic flow with no body forces. The faces parallel to the shock carry all the flux; on the other faces \(\vec V\cdot d\vec S=0\).

\[ \rho_1 u_1 = \rho_2 u_2 \tag{9.2} \]
\[ w_1 = w_2 \tag{9.5} \]
\[ p_1 + \rho_1 u_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 u_2^2 \tag{9.7} \]
\[ h_1 + \frac{u_1^2}{2} = h_2 + \frac{u_2^2}{2} \tag{9.12} \]

Continuity, tangential momentum, normal momentum and energy. The tangential velocity does not change across the shock, and the tangential component w never appears in (9.2), (9.7) or (9.12).

Changes across an oblique shock are governed only by the component of velocity normal to the wave. The normal-shock relations apply unchanged with M1 replaced by Mn,1.
Derivation from the integral conservation laws (notes pp. 77–80)
1

Continuity: on face a, \(\vec V_1=-u_1\hat n+w_1\hat t\) and \(d\vec S=\hat n\,dS\), so \(\oint_a\rho\vec V\cdot d\vec S=-\rho_1u_1A\). On face d the flux is \(+\rho_2u_2A\). Faces b, c, e, f are parallel to the flow.

\[ \oint_S\rho\vec V\cdot d\vec S=-\rho_1u_1A+\rho_2u_2A=0\;\Rightarrow\;\rho_1u_1=\rho_2u_2 \]
2

Momentum is a vector equation, \(\oint_S(\rho\vec V\cdot d\vec S)\vec V=-\oint_S p\,d\vec S\). Its tangential component has no pressure contribution, because the pressure forces on b and f (and on c and e) cancel:

\[ -(\rho_1u_1A)\,w_1+(\rho_2u_2A)\,w_2=0\;\xrightarrow{(9.2)}\;w_1=w_2 \]
3

The normal component of momentum:

\[ -(\rho_1u_1A)\,u_1+(\rho_2u_2A)\,u_2=-(-p_1A+p_2A)\;\Rightarrow\;p_1+\rho_1u_1^2=p_2+\rho_2u_2^2 \]
4

Energy, \(\oint_S\rho\left(e+\tfrac{V^2}{2}\right)\vec V\cdot d\vec S=-\oint_S p\vec V\cdot d\vec S\), with h = e + p/ρ and (9.2):

\[ h_1+\frac{V_1^2}{2}=h_2+\frac{V_2^2}{2}=h_0 \tag{9.11} \]

Total enthalpy, and for a calorically perfect gas total temperature T0, is constant across the shock. Since V² = u² + w² and w1 = w2, \(V_1^2-V_2^2=u_1^2-u_2^2\), which gives (9.12).

Equations (9.2), (9.7) and (9.12) are exactly the normal-shock equations (8.2), (8.6) and (8.10). The same algebra therefore gives the oblique-shock jumps in terms of the normal Mach number:

\[ M_{n,1}=M_1\sin\beta \tag{9.13} \]
\[ M_{n,2}^2=\frac{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_{n,1}^2}{\gamma M_{n,1}^2-\frac{\gamma-1}{2}} \tag{9.14} \]
\[ \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}=\frac{(\gamma+1)M_{n,1}^2}{2+(\gamma-1)M_{n,1}^2} \tag{9.15} \]
\[ \frac{p_2}{p_1}=1+\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}\left(M_{n,1}^2-1\right) \tag{9.16} \]
\[ \frac{T_2}{T_1}=\frac{p_2}{p_1}\,\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2} \tag{9.17} \]
\[ M_2=\frac{M_{n,2}}{\sin(\beta-\theta)} \tag{9.18} \]

Section 9.2 · The vital chart

The θ–β–M relation

Equation (9.18) needs θ, but θ is not a third independent parameter. From the shock geometry, \(\tan\beta=u_1/w_1\) and \(\tan(\beta-\theta)=u_2/w_2\) (9.19, 9.20). Dividing, and using w1 = w2 with continuity:

\[ \frac{\tan(\beta-\theta)}{\tan\beta}=\frac{u_2}{u_1}=\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}=\frac{2+(\gamma-1)M_1^2\sin^2\beta}{(\gamma+1)M_1^2\sin^2\beta} \tag{9.22} \]

Trigonometric rearrangement gives θ explicitly as a unique function of M1 and β:

\[ \tan\theta = 2\cot\beta\;\frac{M_1^2\sin^2\beta-1}{M_1^2\left(\gamma+\cos2\beta\right)+2} \tag{9.23} \]

Five things the chart tells you

  1. Maximum deflection. For each M1 there is a θmax. If the geometry demands θ > θmax, no straight attached shock exists; a curved shock detaches from the corner or the nose. θmax grows with M1, but only to sin−1(1/γ) = 45.58° as M1 → ∞ (γ = 1.4).
  2. Two solutions. For θ < θmax there are two straight shocks. The smaller β is the weak shock, the larger the strong shock. Nature usually picks the weak one; the strong solution needs a high downstream back pressure.
  3. θ = 0. Then β = 90° (a normal shock) or β = μ (a Mach wave). Neither deflects the streamlines.
  4. Raise M1 at fixed θ: β decreases and the shock becomes stronger. Lower M1 far enough and the shock detaches; for θ = 20° this happens below M1 = 1.84.
  5. Raise θ at fixed M1: β increases and the shock becomes stronger, until θ > θmax and the shock detaches; for M1 = 2 that is θ > 22.97°.

The weak branch gives supersonic M2 except in a thin band just below θmax, between the dotted M2 = 1 line and the dashed θmax line. The strong branch is always subsonic.

Supersonic inlets. A normal shock ahead of a pitot inlet causes a large total-pressure loss. In an oblique-shock inlet, a central cone or ramp creates an oblique shock, and the flow then passes through a relatively weak normal shock at the lip. For the same flight condition the total-pressure loss is smaller, so the engine produces more thrust. This is why most modern supersonic aircraft use oblique-shock inlets.

Section 9.4

Shock reflection and intersection

A shock that hits a solid wall must reflect, because the flow behind it, deflected by θ, would otherwise pass through the wall. The reflected shock turns the flow back through θ so that it runs parallel to the upper wall. The reflected shock is weaker than the incident shock, because M2 < M1, and it makes a smaller angle Φ with the wall. If M2 is too low for a straight attached shock to turn the flow by θ (θ > θmax(M2)), regular reflection is impossible. A normal shock, the Mach stem, forms at the wall instead. This is Mach reflection; its wave pattern requires numerical solution.

When a right-running and a left-running shock intersect, each continues as a refracted shock. The two streams behind them must have the same pressure and the same direction, but they have crossed different shocks, so their entropy, density and speed differ. They are separated by a slip line: p4 = p4′ and the flow directions match, while ρ4 ≠ ρ4′ and V4 ≠ V4′. When two left-running shocks intersect, they merge into a single stronger shock, and a weak reflected wave and a slip line adjust the flow so that both streams leave in the same direction.

Section 9.6

Prandtl–Meyer expansion waves

The expansion fan at a convex corner is a continuous region that can be visualized as an infinite number of Mach waves, each at the local Mach angle μ to the local flow direction. The fan is bounded by the forward Mach line at μ1 to the upstream flow and the rearward Mach line at μ2 to the downstream flow. Since ds = 0 across each Mach wave, the expansion is isentropic: p0 and T0 are constant through it, in direct contrast to the entropy rise across a shock.

The problem: given the upstream flow (region 1) and the deflection θ, find the downstream flow (region 2).

Derivation of the Prandtl–Meyer function (notes pp. 94–97)
1

Consider one Mach wave that turns the flow by an infinitesimal dθ. Momentum across the wave keeps the tangential velocity: \(V_{t1}=V_{t2}\), while \(V_{n1}\neq V_{n2}\). With \(V_t=V\cos\mu\) upstream and \(V_t=(V+dV)\cos(\mu+d\theta)\) downstream:

\[ \frac{V+dV}{V}=\frac{\cos\mu}{\cos(\mu+d\theta)}=\frac{\cos\mu}{\cos\mu\cos d\theta-\sin\mu\sin d\theta}\approx\frac{1}{1-d\theta\tan\mu} \]
2

The binomial expansion \((1-x)^{-1}\approx1+x\) for |x| ≪ 1 gives \(dV/V=d\theta\tan\mu\). With \(\tan\mu=1/\sqrt{M^2-1}\):

\[ d\theta=\sqrt{M^2-1}\,\frac{dV}{V} \]

This differential equation describes the flow inside the expansion wave exactly.

3

Express dV/V in terms of M. With V = aM, \(dV/V=da/a+dM/M\). From \(a_0^2/a^2=T_0/T=1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\):

\[ \frac{da}{a}=-\frac{\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M\,dM}{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{dV}{V}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2}\,\frac{dM}{M} \]
4

Integrate from M1 to M2:

\[ \theta=\int_{M_1}^{M_2}\frac{\sqrt{M^2-1}}{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2}\,\frac{dM}{M}=\nu(M_2)-\nu(M_1) \]
\[ \nu(M)=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}\tan^{-1}\!\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}\left(M^2-1\right)}-\tan^{-1}\!\sqrt{M^2-1} \]
\[ \theta=\nu(M_2)-\nu(M_1) \]

ν is the Prandtl–Meyer function; ν(1) = 0. Appendix C tabulates ν and μ against M.

  1. For the given M1, find ν(M1).
  2. Compute ν(M2) = ν(M1) + θ.
  3. Invert ν to find M2.
  4. The wave is isentropic, so p0 and T0 are constant:
\[ \frac{T_2}{T_1}=\frac{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_1^2}{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_2^2},\qquad \frac{p_2}{p_1}=\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}} \]

Examples 9.10 and 9.11

Isentropic compression versus a sharp corner

Turn a supersonic flow into itself gradually, along a smooth concave surface, and each small turn sends out a weak compression Mach wave. The waves are isentropic, so the same Prandtl–Meyer relation applies with the sign reversed:

\[ \text{expansion: } \theta=\nu(M_2)-\nu(M_1),\qquad \text{compression: } \theta=\nu(M_1)-\nu(M_2) \]

Downstream, the compression waves converge and coalesce into a shock away from the wall. Near the wall the compression stays isentropic. Turn the same flow through the same angle at a sharp corner and it crosses a single oblique shock instead.

Section 9.7

Shock-expansion theory: supersonic airfoils

Every surface of a sharp-edged airfoil is flat, so the pressure on each face is uniform. March the flow from the leading edge: on each face, decide whether the flow is turned into itself (oblique shock) or away from itself (Prandtl–Meyer expansion), find the face pressure, and integrate the pressures into forces. Region 1 is the freestream, with M1 and p1, and

\[ q_1=\tfrac12\rho_1V_1^2=\tfrac12\frac{\gamma p_1}{a_1^2}V_1^2=\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\,p_1M_1^2 \]
In an inviscid supersonic flow over a two-dimensional body the drag is finite: d’Alembert’s paradox does not hold. Shock waves are always dissipative, drag-producing mechanisms. This drag is called wave drag, cd,w.

Flat plate at angle of attack

The upper surface turns the flow away from itself by α: an expansion fan at the leading edge, so p2 < p1. The lower surface turns the flow into itself by α: an oblique shock, so p3 > p1. Per unit span, the pressure difference acts normal to the plate:

\[ R'=(p_3-p_2)\,c,\qquad L'=R'\cos\alpha,\qquad D'=R'\sin\alpha \]
\[ c_l=\frac{2}{\gamma M_1^2}\left(\frac{p_3}{p_1}-\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)\cos\alpha,\qquad c_d=\frac{2}{\gamma M_1^2}\left(\frac{p_3}{p_1}-\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)\sin\alpha \]

Hence cd/cl = tan α. At the trailing edge the two streams meet again: the upper flow turns back through a shock, the lower flow through an expansion, and a slip line separates them.

Wedge with a blunt base

Half-angle δ, chord c, face length ℓ = c/cos δ, base height t = 2c tan δ. The upper face (region 2) turns the flow by δ − α and the lower face (region 3) by δ + α. The base sits in separated flow; take pb = p1, as in Example 9.6. In body axes, with N′ normal to the chord and A′ along it:

\[ N'=(p_3-p_2)\,c,\qquad A'=(p_2+p_3)\,\frac{t}{2}-p_b\,t \]
\[ L'=N'\cos\alpha-A'\sin\alpha,\qquad D'=N'\sin\alpha+A'\cos\alpha \]

At α = 0, p2 = p3 and the drag of Example 9.6 follows:

\[ D'=2c\tan\delta\,(p_2-p_1),\qquad c_{d,w}=\frac{4\tan\delta}{\gamma M_1^2}\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}-1\right) \]

For a given geometry this inviscid wave-drag coefficient depends only on the Mach number.

Symmetric diamond wedge

Half-angle ε, side length ℓ, thickness t: sin ε = (t/2)/ℓ, and t/c = tan ε with c = 2√(ℓ² − t²/4). Regions 2 and 3 are the front and rear faces on top; 2′ and 3′ are the front and rear faces underneath. For |α| < ε the front faces see shocks; the shoulders always expand the flow through 2ε.

At α = 0, symmetry gives L′ = 0 and

\[ D'=2\ell\sin\varepsilon\,(p_2-p_3)=(p_2-p_3)\,t \]
\[ c_l=0,\qquad c_d=\frac{2}{\gamma M_1^2}\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}-\frac{p_3}{p_1}\right)\tan\varepsilon \]

At angle of attack, in body axes:

\[ N'=\left[(p_{2'}-p_2)+(p_{3'}-p_3)\right]\frac{c}{2} \]
\[ A'=\left[(p_2+p_{2'})-(p_3+p_{3'})\right]\frac{t}{2} \]
\[ L'=N'\cos\alpha-A'\sin\alpha,\qquad D'=N'\sin\alpha+A'\cos\alpha \]

Region by region

Trailing-edge waves and the slip line are computed by matching pressure and flow direction behind the trailing edge. They do not change any surface pressure.
