Section 9.2 · Solving the θ–β–M relation for β

How β is found: three methods

Equation (9.23) gives θ directly from M1 and β, but not β from M1 and θ. There are three ways to find β, and for the inputs above they are applied side by side. All three must agree, and they do, to within the precision of each method.

1. Reading the chart

2. Bisection, used by this calculator

For a given M1, θ(β) from (9.23) rises from zero at the Mach angle μ to θmax and falls back to zero at 90°. Each branch therefore contains exactly one β for each θ < θmax. Bisection keeps an interval known to contain it and halves the interval at every step: the weak solution lies between μ and β at θmax, the strong one between β at θmax and 90°. It always converges, but it needs many steps.

3. Closed form (Rudd and Lewis, 1998)

Written in terms of tan β, Equation (9.23) is a cubic, and a cubic can be solved in closed form. With c = tan2μ, its three roots follow from

\[ a=\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{2}+\frac{\gamma+1}{2}\,c\right)\tan\theta \]
\[ b=\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{2}+\frac{\gamma+3}{2}\,c\right)\tan\theta \]
\[ k=27a^2c+9ab-2 \]
\[ d=\sqrt{\frac{4\,(1-3ab)^3}{k^2}-1} \]
\[ \tan\beta=\frac{b+9ac}{2\,(1-3ab)}-\frac{d\,k\,\tan\phi_n}{6a\,(1-3ab)} \]
\[ \phi_n=\frac{n\pi}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\tan^{-1}\frac{1}{d},\qquad n=0,1,2 \]

Only two of the three roots are physical, with β between μ and 90°: the smaller is the weak solution and the larger the strong one. The third root is not a shock. The formula needs no iteration, but it has edge cases of its own: it is undefined at θ = 0, where a = 0, and as θ → 0 the weak root loses digits to cancellation. This closed form was used by the first version of this calculator in 2025 (L. Rudd and M. J. Lewis, “Comparison of shock calculation methods,” Journal of Aircraft, 35(4), 1998).

Reference

Relations used

The θ–β–M relation

\[ \tan\theta = 2\cot\beta\;\frac{M_1^2\sin^2\beta-1}{M_1^2\left(\gamma+\cos2\beta\right)+2} \tag{9.23} \]

For a given M1, θ rises from 0 at the Mach angle μ = sin−1(1/M1) to a maximum θmax and falls back to 0 at β = 90°, the normal shock. Every θ < θmax therefore has two solutions: the weak one (smaller β, usually observed on wedges and corners) and the strong one. For θ > θmax no attached shock exists and the shock detaches. The calculator finds β, or M1, by bisection on (9.23); the section above compares bisection with reading the chart and with the closed-form solution.

Across the shock

\[ M_{n,1}=M_1\sin\beta \tag{9.13} \]
\[ M_{n,2}^2=\frac{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_{n,1}^2}{\gamma M_{n,1}^2-\frac{\gamma-1}{2}} \tag{9.14} \]
\[ \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}=\frac{(\gamma+1)M_{n,1}^2}{2+(\gamma-1)M_{n,1}^2},\qquad \frac{p_2}{p_1}=1+\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}\left(M_{n,1}^2-1\right) \]
\[ \frac{T_2}{T_1}=\frac{p_2}{p_1}\,\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2},\qquad M_2=\frac{M_{n,2}}{\sin(\beta-\theta)} \tag{9.18} \]

These are the normal-shock relations (9.15)–(9.17) applied to Mn,1; the tangential velocity is unchanged across the shock.
